# LinkedIn — wildfire losses

**Hook:** When a wildfire destroys a home, the loss doesn't stop at the property. New research finds that occupants of destroyed homes earn less for three years after the fire, a cumulative loss equal to 30% of a year's income — about $38,000 — on top of the cost of rebuilding.

**Why it matters:**

Isolating that effect is difficult in practice, since the households living in a fire's path differ from the country at large in ways that have nothing to do with the fire. By linking address-level damage assessments from more than 100 U.S. wildfires to tax and Census records, the researchers were able to follow the same individuals from year to year and compare a household whose home burned against one inside the same fire perimeter whose home survived. Both groups were on the same income path beforehand, and only the households that lost a home fall behind afterward, which supports reading the drop as a consequence of losing the home rather than of living through the fire.

The effects outlast the households themselves. Four years after a fire, half of destroyed addresses are still unoccupied, and the homes that have been rebuilt and reoccupied house higher-income residents than lived there before.

This is one set of large western wildfires between 2003 and 2022, and the study doesn't settle why income falls — employment barely moves, which points toward fewer hours worked rather than lost jobs. What it does establish is that the financial cost of losing a home runs well past the rebuilding bill, which matters for anyone designing recovery assistance or weighing what it is worth to help households protect a home before a fire.

**Credit:** New EIL research by Patrick Baylis and colleagues.
